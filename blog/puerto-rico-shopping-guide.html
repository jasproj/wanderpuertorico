Puerto Rico Shopping Guide: What to Buy & Best Souvenirs

Updated August 2026 · 10-minute read

Puerto Rico has the best shopping advantage in the Caribbean: it's a U.S. territory, so everything you buy comes home with no customs, no duty, and no agricultural restrictions. Your rum, coffee, and hot sauce fly back in checked luggage as if you went to Florida. That removes every headache that makes souvenir shopping on other islands feel risky.

But the real value isn't brand-name goods at the mall — it's locally made products you can't get anywhere else. This guide covers what's actually worth buying, where to find it, and what to skip.

The Best Things to Buy

Puerto Rican Coffee

Puerto Rico grows some of the best coffee in the world — seriously. The central mountain climate (cool nights, volcanic soil, shade-grown) produces beans that compete with anything from Colombia or Ethiopia. Bring home whole beans from a mountain hacienda for the real thing. Major brands like Alto Grande and Yaucono are good. Single-origin from Adjuntas, Jayuya, or Lares is better. Expect $8-$15 for a supermarket bag, $15-$30 for hacienda single-origin.

Rum

Puerto Rico produces 70% of the rum sold in the U.S. — but the bottles worth bringing home are the ones you won't find on the mainland. Ron del Barrilito (2- and 3-star) is the prestige pick — a small-batch rum aged in sherry barrels, hard to find outside the island. Don Q is the local favorite (Puerto Ricans drink Don Q, not Bacardi). Pitorro is Puerto Rican moonshine — if you find it at a market or through a local, it's a unique souvenir. Skip Bacardi — you can buy it anywhere.

Hot Sauce (Pique)

Pique is Puerto Rico's signature hot sauce — peppers steeped in vinegar with garlic, pineapple, or other aromatics. Every restaurant has a different version. You can buy artisan bottles at farmers markets and specialty shops ($5-$12). It's the one condiment that will make you think about Puerto Rico every time you cook.

Vejigante Masks

The most iconic Puerto Rican craft. Colorful horned masks used in traditional festivals in Ponce (papier-mâché) and Loíza (coconut shell). Handmade by local artisans, each one unique. Prices range from $20 for small tourist versions to $100+ for large, hand-painted originals from master mask-makers. A real one is worth the money — it's wearable art with cultural significance.

Santos (Carved Saints)

Hand-carved wooden saint figures — a folk art tradition dating back 500 years. Artisans carve them from cedar or mahogany, then paint them by hand. They range from small ($25-$50) to museum-quality pieces ($200+). Old San Juan galleries and Ponce shops carry the best selection. Each santero (carver) has a distinctive style.

Mundillo Lace

Bobbin lace made by hand — a tradition brought from Spain and now uniquely Puerto Rican. You'll find it as doilies, fans, ornaments, and jewelry. The town of Moca is the mundillo capital. At markets and craft fairs, you can sometimes watch artisans working the bobbins. Small pieces start around $15; intricate work runs much higher.

Local Chocolate

Puerto Rico's cacao revival has produced some excellent bean-to-bar chocolate. Look for Chocobar Cortés (Old San Juan — you can drink it there and buy bars to go), and small-batch makers at farmers markets. Puerto Rican cacao has a distinctive fruity flavor. Bars run $6-$12.

Spices & Seasonings

The flavors that make Puerto Rican food taste like Puerto Rican food: adobo (garlic-pepper seasoning), sazón (annatto-based spice blend), sofrito (aromatic paste — buy frozen packs at the supermarket). These are cheap ($2-$5) and will upgrade your home cooking immediately. Available at any supermarket.

Where to Shop

Old San Juan — Artisan Shops & Boutiques

The best concentration of unique shops on the island. Calle del Cristo and Calle San Francisco are the main shopping streets. Mix of artisan galleries, jewelry stores, clothing boutiques, and souvenir shops. Quality varies wildly — the shops on side streets tend to have more authentic goods than the ones on the main drag. For coffee, rum, and hot sauce, hit the specialty food shops rather than the generic souvenir stores.

Santurce — Art & Independent Designers

The creative district. Local designers, street art prints, handmade jewelry, and one-of-a-kind clothing. Walk Calle Loíza for boutiques and cafes. The Saturday morning art market (when running) has emerging artists selling prints, ceramics, and textiles. Less tourist-oriented than Old San Juan — you'll find things here that don't exist anywhere else.

Plaza del Mercado (Santurce Market)

The local market — produce, spices, dried goods, and food vendors. This is where to buy sofrito, adobo, sazón, dried beans, and local produce at real prices. It's a working market, not a tourist attraction (though it becomes a nightlife spot on weekends). Come in the morning for the best shopping experience.

Plaza Las Américas

The largest mall in the Caribbean. Standard mainland brands (Macy's, JCPenney, Zara, H&M) plus Puerto Rican stores. Not unique to the island, but useful if you need something specific. No sales tax on clothing in Puerto Rico — so brand-name clothes are slightly cheaper than the mainland. Air-conditioned refuge on hot days.

Ponce — Downtown Craft Shops

The southern capital has excellent vejigante mask shops, santos carvers, and a more traditional arts scene than San Juan. Walk the plaza area and the streets around the Museo de Arte de Ponce. Ponce artisans have been making masks for generations — the quality and variety is better here than anywhere else. Worth a day trip from San Juan.

Coffee Country Haciendas

Drive to the central mountains (Adjuntas, Jayuya, Lares, Maricao) and buy coffee directly from the haciendas that grow it. Hacienda San Pedro, Hacienda Muñoz, and Hacienda Tres Ángeles all sell their beans on-site, often freshly roasted. You'll pay hacienda prices ($12-$25/bag) but the quality and the experience — seeing where it's grown — can't be matched. Combine with a road trip.

Rum Distilleries

Casa Bacardí (Cataño, across the bay from Old San Juan) — the big tourist experience with tastings and a gift shop. Ron del Barrilito (Bayamón) — small-batch, harder to tour but their rum is the one locals prize. Don Q / Destilería Serrallés (Ponce) — tours and tastings at the source of Puerto Rico's #1 rum. Buy bottles at the distillery for the best selection of limited editions.

What to Skip

Tourist Trap Warnings

Generic "Puerto Rico" t-shirts and keychains: Mass-produced, made overseas, available at every cruise port on earth. If it says "I ❤ PR" and costs $8, it was made in China.

Bacardi rum: You can buy it at any liquor store on the mainland for the same price or less. Bring home Don Q or Ron del Barrilito instead.

Overpriced cigars: Puerto Rico is not a cigar island the way Cuba or the DR is. Some shops sell low-quality cigars at inflated prices. If you want cigars, buy from a reputable tobacconist, not a random Old San Juan storefront.

Machine-made "handmade" crafts: If a vejigante mask is $5 and looks perfectly uniform, it's factory-made. Real handmade masks have individual character and cost $20+.

Practical Tips

No Customs, No Duty

Puerto Rico is a U.S. territory. There are zero customs or duty restrictions when flying back to the mainland. No forms to fill out, no declarations, no agricultural inspections. Your rum, coffee, food, and crafts come home exactly like a purchase from any U.S. state. This is PR's biggest shopping advantage over every other Caribbean island.

Packing Rum

TSA carry-on rules apply (3.4 oz / 100 ml per container). Pack rum bottles in checked luggage, wrapped in clothes or bubble wrap. There's no limit on how many bottles you can bring. Some rum shops will pack and ship for you — ask before buying if you're buying in bulk.

Sales Tax

Puerto Rico has an 11.5% sales tax (IVU) on most goods. No sales tax on unprepared food (supermarket groceries) and some clothing. Prices in shops usually don't include tax — it's added at checkout. Budget accordingly for big purchases.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the best souvenirs from Puerto Rico?

The best souvenirs are Puerto Rican coffee (Alto Grande, Yaucono, or single-origin from mountain haciendas), local rum (Don Q or Ron del Barrilito — not Bacardi), hot sauce (Pique), handmade vejigante masks from Ponce or Loíza, mundillo lace, santos carvings, artisan jewelry, and locally made chocolate.

Where is the best shopping in Puerto Rico?

Old San Juan for artisan shops and boutiques along Calle del Cristo and Calle San Francisco. Santurce for art and independent designers. Plaza Las Américas (largest mall in the Caribbean) for mainstream brands. Local markets like Plaza del Mercado in Santurce for food items. Ponce's downtown for regional crafts.

Is Puerto Rico a good place to shop?

Yes, especially for unique local goods. As a U.S. territory, there's no customs or duty when bringing items back to the mainland. Prices are comparable to the mainland U.S. for brand-name goods. The real value is in locally made items — coffee, rum, art, and crafts — that you can't find elsewhere.

Can I bring rum back from Puerto Rico?

Yes — and more than you'd think. Since Puerto Rico is a U.S. territory, there are no customs limits for travelers returning to the mainland. You can bring as much rum as you can carry in checked luggage. TSA liquid rules apply to carry-on (3.4 oz limit), so pack bottles in checked bags.

What food can I bring back from Puerto Rico?

Coffee (whole bean or ground), hot sauce (Pique), local spices and seasonings (adobo, sazón), chocolate, candy (coconut candies, besitos de coco), dried goods, and packaged snacks. Since Puerto Rico is U.S. territory, there are no agricultural restrictions like you'd face returning from international destinations.

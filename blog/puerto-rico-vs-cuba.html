Puerto Rico vs Cuba: Costs, Culture & Which to Pick in 2026

Both are Caribbean islands with deep Spanish colonial roots, incredible music, and beautiful beaches. But they couldn't be more different to actually visit. Puerto Rico is a U.S. territory — fly domestic, book anything online, pay with your credit card. Cuba has travel restrictions, no U.S. credit cards, and a completely different set of logistics. Here's the honest breakdown.

Can Americans Even Go to Cuba?

This is the first question, and it changes everything. U.S. citizens cannot legally travel to Cuba for tourism. You must travel under one of several OFAC-licensed categories — the most common being "Support for the Cuban People," which requires staying at private homes, eating at private restaurants, and maintaining a full-time schedule of activities that support Cuban citizens. You need documentation of your itinerary. Standard beach vacations are not permitted.

Puerto Rico has zero restrictions. It's a U.S. territory. Fly with your driver's license, book hotels on any site, use your cell phone, and go wherever you want.

Passport & Logistics

Puerto Rico: no passport needed, domestic flights, U.S. dollars, U.S. cell plans work, book everything online. Cuba: valid U.S. passport required, Cuban tourist visa (tarjeta de turista) required, must select an authorized travel category, U.S. credit and debit cards do not work in Cuba (bring euros or Canadian dollars to exchange), limited internet access, and most booking must happen through specialized agencies or direct contact with casas particulares.

Cuba is significantly cheaper day-to-day — casas particulares (private home stays) run $30–$80/night, meals at paladares (private restaurants) are $5–$15 per person. But flights are expensive, logistics eat time, and you can't just swipe a card. Puerto Rico costs more per day but is frictionless to book and pay for.

Beaches

Cuba's Strengths

Varadero is Cuba's flagship beach — a 20-kilometer stretch of white sand and turquoise water that's genuinely spectacular. Cayo Coco and Cayo Santa María offer resort-style beach experiences. The beaches are less crowded than almost anywhere in the Caribbean because fewer Americans visit.

Puerto Rico's Strengths

Flamenco Beach in Culebra is consistently ranked among the world's best. Rincón has real surf. The island has mangrove-lined bays, secluded southern coves, and offshore island beaches. And Puerto Rico has something Cuba simply doesn't: bioluminescent bays — three of them, including the brightest on Earth.

Culture & History

Cuba's Draw

  • Havana: A city frozen in time — classic American cars, crumbling colonial architecture, the Malecón seawall, and live salsa and son music on every corner. There is genuinely nothing like walking through Havana Vieja.
  • Music: Cuba is the birthplace of son, mambo, cha-cha-chá, and modern salsa. Live music is everywhere, and it's world-class.
  • Cigars and rum: Visit tobacco farms in Viñales, tour the Partagás factory, sip aged Havana Club. These are authentic Cuban experiences.
  • Time-capsule experience: The isolation from U.S. commerce has preserved a unique atmosphere that's disappearing gradually.

Puerto Rico's Culture

  • Old San Juan: 500 years of Spanish colonial architecture — beautifully restored, walkable, and vibrant. El Morro and San Cristóbal forts are UNESCO World Heritage Sites.
  • Music: Birthplace of reggaetón, with deep salsa, bomba, and plena traditions. La Placita in Santurce is the best nightlife block in the Caribbean.
  • Food scene: Far more developed than Cuba's — from street lechón to Michelin-level restaurants. Piña coladas were invented here.
  • Accessibility: All of this is bookable, reviewable, and reachable without a specialized travel agency.

Safety & Healthcare

Puerto Rico has U.S. hospitals, 911, and your insurance works. Cuba has well-trained doctors but dated facilities and supply shortages. Foreigners are sent to international clinics that require cash payment upfront. Travel insurance is mandatory for Cuba entry and must be purchased from a Cuban or non-U.S. provider.

Both are generally safe for tourists. Cuba has very low violent crime against visitors. Puerto Rico is safe in tourist zones with standard urban awareness needed in metro San Juan.

Connectivity & Convenience

This is where the gap is widest. Puerto Rico has full U.S. cell service, fast Wi-Fi everywhere, Uber, Google Maps, and every app you use at home. Cuba has extremely limited internet — you'll buy Wi-Fi cards for designated hotspots, cell service is expensive and spotty, and most U.S. apps and services don't work. If staying connected matters, Puerto Rico isn't just better — Cuba is almost offline by comparison.

Which Should You Pick?

Pick Cuba if:

  • You want a culturally immersive, off-the-grid experience unlike anywhere else
  • You're comfortable navigating travel restrictions and OFAC categories
  • Havana's vintage atmosphere, live music, and history are the draw
  • You're okay with cash-only, limited internet, and slower logistics

Pick Puerto Rico if:

  • You want Caribbean culture, history, and beaches without a passport or restrictions
  • Bioluminescent bays, rainforests, and offshore islands are the draw
  • Food, nightlife, and modern convenience matter
  • You want to book and pay the way you normally travel
  • U.S. healthcare, cell service, and infrastructure are important

FAQ

Can Americans travel to Cuba in 2026?

Not for tourism. Travel is only permitted under specific OFAC-licensed categories like "Support for the Cuban People." You need documentation. Puerto Rico has no restrictions.

Do I need a passport for Puerto Rico or Cuba?

Puerto Rico: no. Cuba: yes, plus a Cuban tourist visa and an authorized travel category.

Is Cuba or Puerto Rico cheaper?

Cuba is much cheaper day-to-day, but flights cost more and U.S. cards don't work — bring cash. Puerto Rico's total trip cost is competitive thanks to cheap domestic flights.

Which has better beaches?

Both are excellent. Varadero is stunning. Flamenco Beach is world-class. Puerto Rico also has bioluminescent bays, which Cuba lacks.

Does Cuba have bioluminescent bays?

Some limited bioluminescence near the Bay of Pigs, but nothing comparable to Puerto Rico's three permanent bays.

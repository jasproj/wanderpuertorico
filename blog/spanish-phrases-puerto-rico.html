Essential Spanish Phrases for Puerto Rico

Updated August 2026 · 10-minute read

Puerto Rico is a U.S. territory, so you won't need a passport. But Spanish is the dominant language — especially outside San Juan's tourist corridor. You don't need to be fluent. A handful of phrases will get you through restaurants, directions, shopping, and emergencies. And knowing a few Puerto Rican slang words will earn you instant respect from locals.

Quick reality check

English is common in Old San Juan, Condado, Isla Verde, and resort areas. Once you head to local neighborhoods, small towns, roadside kiosks, or the south/west coast, Spanish becomes essential. Even in tourist areas, starting a conversation in Spanish gets you warmer treatment.

Food words you'll see everywhere

Mofongo — mashed fried plantains (the national dish). Alcapurrias — fried fritters stuffed with meat. Tostones — twice-fried plantain slices. Lechón — spit-roasted pork. Piragua — shaved ice with fruit syrup. Medalla — the local beer. Read our full Puerto Rico food guide.

Puerto Rico says "guagua"

Bus is "guagua" (WAH-wah) in Puerto Rico, not "autobús." If you say autobús, people will understand you but immediately know you're not from here. Same with "china" for orange (the fruit) instead of "naranja."

Puerto Rican Slang You'll Hear

Textbook Spanish will get you understood. But Puerto Rican Spanish has its own vocabulary that you won't find in Duolingo. Here are the words you'll actually hear on the street, at bars, and at festivals.

Boricua

Means "Puerto Rican." Comes from Borinquen, the Taíno name for the island. A term of deep cultural pride. "Yo soy boricua" — I am Puerto Rican.

¡Wepa!

The Puerto Rican exclamation — excitement, celebration, approval. Equivalent to "awesome!" or "hell yeah!" You'll hear it at parties, when a song drops, or when food arrives. Just say it. Locals love it.

Chinchorreo

Bar-hopping at roadside open-air bars called chinchorrros — especially along country roads on weekends. Live music, cheap beer, fried food, and no pretense. The most authentic Puerto Rican social experience. Ask locals: "¿Dónde hay buenos chinchorrros?" and follow their directions.

Chacho / Chacha

Short for "muchacho." Used like "dude" or "man" — "Chacho, ¿qué pasó?" (Dude, what happened?). You'll hear it in every casual conversation.

Bregar

To deal with something, handle it, work through it. "Hay que bregar" — we'll figure it out. Captures the Puerto Rican attitude of resilience and getting things done.

Al garete

Out of control, all over the place, wild. "La fiesta está al garete" — the party is going off. Originally a nautical term (a boat drifting without a rudder).

Jíbaro

A rural Puerto Rican — the mountain farmer, the countryside soul. It's a term of cultural pride tied to tradition, self-reliance, and Puerto Rico's agricultural heritage. Jíbaro food, jíbaro music, jíbaro culture.

Brutal

Means "amazing" or "incredible" — the opposite of what it means in English. "La comida estuvo brutal" — the food was incredible. One of the most common Puerto Rican compliments.

Pronunciation Tips

Puerto Rican Spanish sounds different from Mexican or European Spanish. A few things to expect:

  • Dropped S: "Buenos días" sounds more like "Bueno día." The final S fades or disappears entirely.
  • Swallowed D: "Cansado" (tired) becomes "cansa'o." Middle and final D sounds vanish.
  • R becomes L: "Puerto Rico" can sound like "Puelto Rico" in fast speech. The R at the end of a syllable shifts toward L.
  • Fast pace: Caribbean Spanish is fast. Don't panic. Ask "¿Puede repetir?" (Can you repeat?) — no one minds.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to speak Spanish in Puerto Rico?

No. English is widely spoken in tourist areas like San Juan, Condado, and resort towns. But outside tourist zones — local restaurants, small towns, public transit — Spanish is the primary language. Learning basic phrases shows respect and gets you better service, better prices, and a warmer welcome.

What kind of Spanish do they speak in Puerto Rico?

Puerto Rican Spanish is Caribbean Spanish — faster than Mexican or Castilian Spanish, with dropped S sounds, swallowed D sounds, and unique vocabulary. Puerto Ricans often mix English and Spanish (Spanglish). You'll hear words like "guagua" (bus), "chinchorreo" (bar hopping), and "wepa" (excitement) that you won't find in a textbook.

What does "wepa" mean in Puerto Rico?

Wepa is an exclamation of excitement, joy, or celebration — like "awesome!" or "let's go!" You'll hear it at parties, concerts, and anytime something good happens. It's one of the most distinctly Puerto Rican expressions.

What does "boricua" mean?

Boricua means Puerto Rican. It comes from Borinquen, the Taíno name for Puerto Rico. Calling someone boricua is a term of pride and cultural identity. The island itself is sometimes called Borinquen.

How do you order food in Spanish in Puerto Rico?

Start with "¿Me puede traer...?" (Can you bring me...?) or "Quisiera..." (I'd like...). Ask for the check with "La cuenta, por favor." Tip 15-20% at sit-down restaurants. At roadside kiosks, just point and say "Uno de eso, por favor" (One of those, please).

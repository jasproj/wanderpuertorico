Caves in Puerto Rico: Río Camuy, Cueva Ventana & Underground Adventures

Updated October 2026 · 11 min read

Puerto Rico sits on one of the most extraordinary karst landscapes in the Caribbean. The island's northwestern limestone plateau — a terrain of rounded mogote hills, hidden sinkholes, and underground rivers — contains hundreds of documented cave passages, three publicly accessible show caves, and a river that disappears underground for miles before resurfacing downstream. The centrepiece is Río Camuy Cave Park, which protects one of the longest cave river systems in the Western Hemisphere. Beyond Camuy, visitors can step into Cueva Ventana's dramatic natural window, trace Taíno petroglyphs at Cueva del Indio on the north coast, and swim through underground passages carved by the Tanamá River near Utuado. This guide covers every major cave site on the island, what access each requires, and how to visit safely.

Río Camuy Cave Park: The Main Event

The Río Camuy Cave System is the island's flagship underground attraction — a network of over 200 documented passages and chambers carved by the Camuy River as it dissolves the porous limestone bedrock beneath the northwest plateau. The river disappears underground for several miles before resurfacing, and the cave network it has sculpted over millions of years is among the most extensive in the Western Hemisphere (commonly ranked in the top three by length, though full exploration is ongoing).

The portion open to the public — Río Camuy Cave Park, managed by the Puerto Rico Department of Natural and Environmental Resources (DRNA) — centers on Clara Cave, the main show cave. A trolley descends from the park entrance into the karst valley, then visitors walk a guided route through the chamber. The scale is immediately striking: the main chamber is large enough to fit a 20-story building inside it, with stalactites hanging from ceilings roughly 170 feet overhead. A bat colony of several hundred thousand bats roosts in the upper chambers.

The tour also takes in a sinkhole — a collapsed section of cave ceiling creating a natural pit roughly 65 feet in diameter — with a viewing platform from which you can see the underground river still flowing below. The cave maintains a near-constant temperature of around 70°F year-round.

Practical notes for Río Camuy:
  • Location: PR-129 km 18.9, Camuy municipality — roughly 90 minutes from San Juan via PR-22 west
  • Hours: Typically Wednesday–Sunday, 8:30am–3pm (last trolley mid-afternoon); check DRNA before visiting
  • Reservations: Strongly recommended; limited daily capacity fills quickly on weekends
  • Getting there: No public transit; rental car or a tour with transportation required
  • Footwear: Closed-toe shoes with grip; the cave floor is uneven in places
  • Flash flood closures: The park closes after heavy rain when the underground river rises; check conditions on the day

Cueva Ventana – The Window Cave

Cueva Ventana ("Window Cave") is a privately managed cave on a hillside near Arecibo, about 30 minutes east of Río Camuy. Unlike the Camuy system — a river cave formed by water erosion from below — Cueva Ventana is a hillside cave that ends at a large natural arch opening onto the Río Grande de Arecibo valley. Walking through the cave interior to reach that window is the experience: the transition from limestone tunnel to open sky framing a panorama of karst mogote hills and agricultural valley is one of the more distinctive perspectives in Puerto Rico.

The cave is accessible only with a guide and charges an admission fee (approximately $29 per person for ages 11 and up as of the most recent available pricing; verify with the operator). The guided walk takes roughly 45–60 minutes. Because both Cueva Ventana and Río Camuy are in the northwest karst zone and within 30 minutes of each other, many visitors combine them in a single day.

Cueva del Indio – Taíno Petroglyphs on the Sea Cliffs

Cueva del Indio (Cave of the Indian) sits above sea cliffs on Puerto Rico's north coast near Arecibo. The cave contains petroglyphs — rock carvings made by the Taíno people before Spanish colonization — estimated to be between roughly 1,000 and 2,500 years old, though precise dating of petroglyphs is difficult. The imagery includes human faces, animals, and geometric patterns carved into the cave limestone. Outside the cave, the coastal cliffs display dramatic rock formations sculpted by wave action, and the ocean view from the clifftop is wide and unobstructed.

The site is less developed than Río Camuy — no trolley, no interpretive museum — and a local guide is required both for navigation and to access the petroglyph areas. The cave's relatively raw setting, the archaeology, and the coastal geology make it a good complement to the more polished Camuy and Cueva Ventana experiences for visitors who want a broader picture of Puerto Rico's underground heritage.

Tanamá River – Swimming Through Cave Passages

The Tanamá River in Utuado, in Puerto Rico's mountainous interior, offers a different kind of cave experience: a guided adventure in which you wade, swim, jump, and scramble through a river gorge that passes underground through cave passages. The river has carved its way through the karst over millennia, and certain sections of the route pass through cave chambers where the only way forward is to swim in the river itself — with the cave ceiling close overhead and daylight visible ahead.

This is an active, wet, physically demanding experience. A licensed guide is essential — not just for safety but for navigation, as the route involves reading the river and knowing which channels are passable. Non-swimmers should not attempt the Tanamá cave route. Flash flooding after rain is a real hazard; reputable guide services monitor forecasts and cancel if conditions are unsafe. For the right visitor, it's one of the most visceral natural experiences on the island.

Other Caves Worth Knowing

Guajataca Forest Caves

Guajataca State Forest near Isabela contains caves within its karst terrain that are accessible with a guide but are not managed as show caves with formal tours. These are wild caves; entry requires coordination with the DRNA and an experienced guide. They are appropriate for spelunking-focused visitors who want something beyond the managed park experience.

Mona Island

Mona Island, roughly 50 miles west of the Puerto Rican mainland in the Mona Passage, contains extensive sea-level and coastal caves that hold significant Taíno pictographs. The island is a strict nature reserve managed by the DRNA; visiting requires an advance permit from the department and access by chartered boat from Mayagüez or Joyuda (the crossing takes several hours depending on vessel speed and sea conditions). This is an expedition-level destination for serious adventurers and archaeological enthusiasts, not a day trip.

Cuevas de Cabachuelas and Other Karst Caves

The northwest karst zone contains additional cave systems — including sites near Morovis — that have been explored by local spelunking clubs but are not open to general public access. If you're a serious caver interested in exploring beyond the managed sites, connect with Puerto Rican caving organizations and the DRNA for current access information. Wild cave entry without proper equipment, training, and local knowledge is dangerous.

Cave Safety: What You Need to Know

Flash flooding is the primary danger. Underground rivers can rise extremely rapidly after rain, even rain that falls miles away. Río Camuy Cave Park closes tours proactively when flooding risk exists. Never enter a wild cave or the Tanamá gorge during or shortly after heavy rainfall. Check weather forecasts and heed any closures.
  • Bats: The Río Camuy bat colony is large. The bats are not aggressive and pose no hazard to visitors who stay on the designated path. Do not shine lights directly at roosting bats.
  • Footing: Cave floors are often uneven, slippery from humidity, and irregularly lit. Closed-toe shoes with grip are non-negotiable.
  • Temperature: Cave interiors run 68–72°F year-round — comfortable, but bring a light layer if you run cold.
  • Never go alone: Even at Río Camuy with its guided format, solo cave exploration outside the managed route is prohibited. For any wild or semi-developed site, a guide is essential.
  • Permits: Mona Island requires DRNA permits obtained in advance. Some forest caves require separate authorization. Confirm requirements before your trip.

Planning Your Cave Day

A northwest karst day combining Río Camuy and Cueva Ventana is the most practical introduction to Puerto Rico's cave network. Both sites are within 30 minutes of each other and about 90 minutes from San Juan. Arrive at Camuy when the park opens (around 8:30am) to beat the crowds and catch the first trolley. Finish by mid-morning, drive to Cueva Ventana for a midday tour, then continue north to the coast for a late-afternoon activity near Isabela or Arecibo.

For a more complete underground experience, combine Río Camuy with the Tanamá River in Utuado (about 45 minutes from Camuy). This makes for a long day but covers both the show-cave and adventure-cave sides of Puerto Rico's karst heritage. Book both experiences in advance; the Tanamá river route runs on scheduled departures with limited group sizes.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most famous cave in Puerto Rico?

Río Camuy Cave Park — specifically Clara Cave — is the most visited cave site. It is part of one of the largest cave river systems in the Western Hemisphere, managed by the Puerto Rico DRNA with trolley access and guided tours.

Do I need a guide?

For Río Camuy, the DRNA provides guides as part of the park experience. Cueva Ventana and Cueva del Indio both require a guide by policy. The Tanamá River cave swim requires a licensed adventure guide. Never enter a wild or undeveloped cave without an experienced guide and proper equipment.

Are the caves safe for children?

Río Camuy and Cueva Ventana are accessible to children who can walk on uneven ground and follow guide instructions. The Tanamá river route is not suitable for children or non-swimmers. Check minimum age requirements when booking.

Is Río Camuy Cave Park open year-round?

The park typically operates Wednesday through Sunday, but hours and schedules are subject to change and closures after heavy rain are common. Always confirm current hours and availability on the DRNA website before making the 90-minute drive from San Juan.

Can I swim in Puerto Rico's caves?

Yes — the Tanamá River adventure involves swimming through cave passages, and some karst pools adjacent to caves are swimmable. The main Río Camuy and Cueva Ventana tours are walking experiences only, with no swimming. See Swimming Holes in Puerto Rico for the island's best natural swimming spots.

How do caves in Puerto Rico compare to show caves elsewhere?

Clara Cave at Río Camuy rivals the scale of well-known caves in Kentucky and New Mexico — the main chamber is comparable in volume to some of the largest in the US cave system. The Tanamá river adventure has no close equivalent among commercially operated US cave experiences; it is more analogous to cenote swimming in the Yucatán.

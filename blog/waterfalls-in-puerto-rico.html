Waterfalls in Puerto Rico: La Mina, Gozalandia & the Island's Best Cascades

Updated October 2026 · 12 min read Tours you can book here →

Puerto Rico receives between 60 and 200 inches of rain per year depending on elevation, and that rainfall feeds a network of waterfalls scattered from the mountain peaks of the northeast interior to the cloud forest ridges of the west. The most famous — La Mina Falls in El Yunque National Forest — is 40 minutes from San Juan and draws visitors who come for the paved trail and swimmable pool. But Puerto Rico's waterfall landscape extends far beyond El Yunque: Gozalandia in San Sebastián offers rope swings and deep pools in the west; Doña Juana tumbles through Toro Negro State Forest in the central mountains; and dozens of smaller cascades reward hikers and road-trippers across the island's mountainous interior. This guide covers the most accessible and worthwhile waterfalls by region, with trail conditions, difficulty, swimming access, and the safety information every visitor needs.

El Yunque Waterfalls: The Northeast Quarter

El Yunque National Forest — the only tropical rainforest in the United States National Forest system — receives more rainfall than almost anywhere else on the island, which means its streams run hard year-round. Three main waterfalls are accessible to visitors within the forest, each with a different character and level of effort required.

La Mina Falls

La Mina is the most visited waterfall in Puerto Rico. It sits at the end of the La Mina Trail — a paved, 0.7-mile route descending through dense rainforest to a cascade that drops roughly 35 feet into a pool deep enough to swim in. The paved surface makes it accessible to most fitness levels, though the descent is steep enough that the return climb will wind less-fit visitors. The pool at the base is cold, clear, and crowded on weekends.

Trail status: El Yunque's trail system has experienced periodic closures due to ongoing recovery work following Hurricane Maria (2017) and subsequent storm damage. Conditions change. Always check current status with the USFS El Yunque Recreation Area before visiting; trails can close without extended notice.

La Coca Falls

La Coca Falls is the most visible waterfall on the island for anyone driving PR-191 into El Yunque — it tumbles directly alongside the road at a pullout about 3 miles above the forest entrance. The cascade is tall (roughly 85 feet across several tiers) and impressive, especially after rain. This is a viewpoint and photograph stop only; the falls drop directly over the road cut and there is no safe approach to the base. Its accessibility makes it the most-photographed waterfall in Puerto Rico.

Juan Diego Falls

Juan Diego Falls requires more effort than La Mina but rewards visitors with a quieter setting and a swimmable pool at a waterfall that sees significantly fewer visitors. The Juan Diego Trail branches off from the Big Tree Trail and descends through old-growth sierra palm forest to the falls and its pool. Moderate fitness required; wet, root-covered trail sections demand careful footing. Check trail accessibility at the visitor center on the day you go.

Gozalandia & San Sebastián: The West's Waterfall Hub

Gozalandia in San Sebastián is Puerto Rico's most-visited non-El Yunque waterfall. The site — privately owned and managed, with a paid admission — features a two-tier waterfall system with deep pools at each level and the rope swing at the upper pool that has made it famous on social media. The setting is lush: bamboo groves, vine-covered boulders, and the cool water of the Río Culebrinas running through a gorge in the western foothills.

Gozalandia is about 90 minutes from San Juan via PR-22 west. The privately managed entry means the site is better maintained than many forest waterfalls — restrooms, a parking area, and a small food stand are available on-site. Weekends draw large crowds; weekday mornings offer the pools with far fewer people. No rental cars, no problem: some tours from San Juan include Gozalandia, though it's more commonly reached independently.

Salto Collazo

Salto Collazo is a second waterfall in the San Sebastián area, located nearby and often paired with a Gozalandia visit. It is less developed and less crowded than Gozalandia, with its own pool accessible via a short trail. Local knowledge helps here — the access trail is not as clearly marked as Gozalandia's entrance. For visitors who want a quieter experience in the same region, Salto Collazo is worth the minor extra navigation.

Interior Waterfalls: Toro Negro & the Central Mountains

Doña Juana Falls

Doña Juana Falls sits near Orocovis in Toro Negro State Forest — Puerto Rico's largest state forest by area, spanning the central mountain ridge near Cerro de Punta, the island's highest peak. The falls drop roughly 200 feet in multiple tiers and feed a pool at the bottom. The access trail is maintained but more rugged than El Yunque's paved routes; moderate fitness and proper footwear are essential. The central mountains are noticeably cooler than the coast — temperatures at Toro Negro elevation can be 10–15°F lower than San Juan, which makes the waterfall pool feel cold even on a warm day.

Doña Juana is best reached from Orocovis or from PR-143 (the Ruta Panorámica), Puerto Rico's scenic mountain highway. This is not a quick detour — factor in driving time on winding mountain roads and allow a full day for a Toro Negro visit. The reward is a waterfall that relatively few tourists reach, in a forest landscape quite different from El Yunque's lowland-to-peak transition.

Southeast: Charco Azul Area

The Patillas area in southeastern Puerto Rico is less frequently covered in waterfall guides but contains freshwater pools fed by the mountain streams descending from the Carite State Forest to the coast. The Charco Azul area near Patillas offers a vivid blue-green pool fed by a small cascade — striking against the surrounding vegetation. This region is best explored with local knowledge; roads into the interior are small and often unmarked on navigation apps. A local guide or thorough research before departure is recommended.

When to Go & Flash Flood Warning

Puerto Rico's waterfalls flow year-round, but their character and safety depend heavily on recent rainfall. The dry season (December–April) typically delivers the safest conditions for swimming and trail access: water levels are manageable, trails are drier, and the leptospirosis risk is lower. The wet season (May–November) produces more dramatic water volume and lush green surroundings, but also raises the risk significantly.

Flash floods kill. Puerto Rico's mountain streams can rise several feet within minutes after rain — even rain falling miles upstream that you cannot see from the waterfall itself. Never visit a waterfall pool during rain or within 24–48 hours of significant rainfall. If the water looks unusually cloudy or brown, leave immediately. This applies to every waterfall on this list without exception.

Leptospirosis: The Hidden Risk

Leptospirosis is a bacterial infection transmitted when water or soil contaminated by infected animal urine enters the body through cuts, the mouth, eyes, or nose. In Puerto Rico, the bacteria are present in freshwater systems year-round, with the concentration rising after heavy rain. Symptoms resemble flu: fever, headache, muscle pain, and in severe cases, organ damage.

  • Avoid swimming in any freshwater within 24–48 hours of heavy rain
  • Cover any open cuts before entering waterfall pools
  • Do not swallow waterfall pool water
  • If you develop flu-like symptoms 2–30 days after freshwater swimming, seek medical attention and mention the exposure

What to Bring

  • Footwear: Water shoes or closed-toe trail shoes with grip — waterfall trails are wet and slippery
  • Change of clothes: A dry set in a waterproof bag for after swimming
  • Water: At least 2 liters per person; forest humidity masks sweat
  • Sunscreen: For open sections and the drive out
  • Insect repellent: Mosquitoes and no-see-ums are active in the forest understory
  • Offline maps: Cell signal is unreliable in El Yunque and the central mountains
  • Cash: Gozalandia and some other managed sites charge admission and may not accept cards

Guided vs. Self-Guided

El Yunque's main waterfalls — La Mina and La Coca — are accessible without a guide on paved, well-marked trails. Guided tours from San Juan are available and worthwhile if you don't have a rental car or want interpretation; see El Yunque Waterfall Tours for guided options. Juan Diego Falls is more easily navigated with a guide or detailed trail information.

Gozalandia is self-guided and independently reachable by rental car. For the interior waterfalls — Doña Juana, the Patillas area charcos, and lesser-known cascades — local knowledge from a guide is strongly advisable. The central mountain roads are slow and the sites are not clearly signposted.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the most popular waterfall in Puerto Rico?

La Mina Falls in El Yunque is the most visited. Its paved trail, proximity to San Juan (about 40 minutes), and swimmable pool make it the default first-waterfall experience for most visitors. Check trail status before going.

Can you swim at La Mina Falls?

Yes — the pool at La Mina is the main draw. It is cold and clear. Swimming is permitted when the trail is open. Avoid the pool within 24–48 hours of significant rain due to flash flood and leptospirosis risk.

Are there waterfalls near San Juan?

El Yunque is 40 minutes east of San Juan and has three accessible waterfalls. Guided tours depart from San Juan daily; see El Yunque Tours for options. Gozalandia is 90 minutes west — better paired with a longer western Puerto Rico day.

Are Puerto Rico waterfalls safe to visit?

With proper timing and awareness, yes. The main risks are flash floods (do not visit during or 24–48 hours after heavy rain) and leptospirosis (avoid swimming after rain). Always wear proper footwear and tell someone your itinerary before heading to a remote waterfall.

What is Gozalandia and is it worth visiting?

Gozalandia is a privately managed two-tier waterfall and pool complex in San Sebastián, western Puerto Rico. It is worth visiting for the rope swing, deep pools, and lush jungle setting — especially on a weekday morning when crowds are thin. It is about 90 minutes from San Juan; see the full guide at Gozalandia: Puerto Rico's Secret Waterfall Playground.
